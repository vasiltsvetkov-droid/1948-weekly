**1. FORCE-VELOCITY PROFILING: THE CORE FRAMEWORK**

**1.1 Theoretical Foundation**

The Force-Velocity (F-V) relationship is the cornerstone of Morin\'s
research program. The framework is grounded in the fundamental muscle
mechanics principle that force output and velocity of contraction are
inversely related. Morin, Samozino, and collaborators translated this
from isolated muscle physiology into whole-body sports performance
assessment.

Key insight: The F-V relationship of the entire neuromuscular system
during athletic movements (sprinting, jumping) follows a linear
relationship between maximal force (F0) and maximal velocity (V0),
producing a hyperbolic power curve with a peak power (Pmax) at
approximately F0/2 and V0/2.

**1.2 Sprint Force-Velocity Profile**

The sprint F-V profile quantifies an athlete\'s neuromuscular mechanical
capabilities during maximal sprint acceleration. Developed
systematically from Morin\'s early work (2011-2016) into a practical
field tool.

**The Ratio of Forces (RF) Concept**

Central innovation: Morin et al. (2011, Med Sci Sports Exerc) identified
that sprint performance is not determined solely by total force
production but by the DIRECTION of force application. RF = horizontal
GRF / total GRF.

-   RFmax (at initial step): typically 55-65% in well-trained sprinters

-   DRF (decline per m/s increase in speed): typically -8 to -10% per
    m/s in untrained; -5 to -7% in elite

-   Elite sprinters maintain higher RF across the acceleration phase,
    not just produce more total force

-   Key paper: Morin et al. 2011 (ref #25) - technical ability of force
    application as determinant of sprint performance

**Optimal F-V Profile**

Samozino et al. (2021, ref #157) formalized the \'optimal mechanical
force-velocity profile\' concept for sprint acceleration. The optimal
profile depends on:

-   Body mass and height

-   Training status and sport

-   The specific performance target (acceleration vs. top speed)

F-V Imbalance = difference between actual profile slope and optimal
slope. Positive imbalance = too force-oriented (needs more velocity
training). Negative imbalance = too velocity-oriented (needs more
force/strength training).

Simple method (Samozino et al. 2016, ref #72): Using only velocity-time
data from a single maximal sprint, compute mechanical outputs via
Newton\'s second law. Requires body mass, height, and sprint distance
data. Validated against force plate measurements with high correlation
(r \> 0.95).

Replication study (Morin et al. 2019, ref #130): Confirmed validity of
the simple method with improved design. Demonstrated that position-time
or velocity-time data from various devices can reliably compute sprint
acceleration kinetics.

Multi-system comparison (Fornasier-Santos et al. 2022, ref #166):
Systematic comparison of position-vs-velocity-time data sources. Key
finding: velocity-time data generally produces more reliable F-V outputs
than position-time data, but both are practically acceptable.

**1.4 Jump Force-Velocity Profile**

Parallel framework developed for vertical jumping (countermovement jump,
squat jump). Quantifies the F-V mechanical profile during lower-limb
explosive extensions.

**Key Variables (Jump)**

-   F0 (jump): Maximal force at zero extension velocity (N/kg)

-   V0 (jump): Maximal extension velocity at zero force (m/s)

-   Pmax (jump): Maximal power during push-off (W/kg)

-   FV-Imbalance (jump): % deviation from optimal profile for jump
    height

Validation: Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2017, ref #84) validated a simple
field method for measuring the F-V-power profile in countermovement
jump. Method uses multiple loaded jumps across a load range (body mass
to \~80% 1RM) to construct the profile.

Critical finding (Morin et al. 2019, ref #129): Jump height alone is NOT
a reliable indicator of lower limb maximal power output. Two athletes
with identical jump heights can have completely different F-V profiles
and therefore different training needs. This invalidates the common
practice of using jump height as a proxy for power.

**1.6 Optimal F-V Profile and Individualized Training**

The concept of an \'optimal\' F-V profile means each athlete has a
theoretically ideal balance between force and velocity capacities for
their target performance. Training should target the DEFICIT side of the
profile, not simply volume-load the existing strengths.

**Training Prescription Logic**

-   Profile is force-deficit (FV \> 0, too velocity-oriented): Add heavy
    strength training, loaded jumps, resisted sprints with high loads

-   Profile is velocity-deficit (FV \< 0, too force-oriented): Add
    plyometrics, assisted sprints, light-load explosive exercises,
    high-velocity work

-   Profile is balanced: Maintain current training mix; monitor for
    seasonal shifts

Evidence for individualized approach (Jimenez-Reyes et al. 2017, ref
#86): Athletes trained according to their F-V imbalance showed
significantly greater improvements in jump height than control groups
using generic training. Effect sizes were large for the individualized
group.

Kinetics of adaptation (Jimenez-Reyes et al. 2019, ref #127): Individual
adaptation kinetics differ substantially between athletes. Some respond
in 4-6 weeks; others need 8-12 weeks. F-V profiling should be repeated
regularly (every 4-6 weeks minimum during intensive training blocks) to
track and adjust.

Morin et al. 2020 (ref #135): Individual adaptation kinetics after heavy
resisted sprint training showed high inter-individual variability.
Recommends individual monitoring rather than group averages.

**1.7 Seasonal and Contextual Changes in F-V Profile**

-   Soccer players show seasonal changes: more force-oriented at end of
    preseason; shift toward velocity-orientation during competitive
    season (Jimenez-Reyes 2020, ref #137)

-   Rugby Sevens: in-season assisted+resisted velocity-based training
    produced individual-specific F-V adaptations (Lahti et al. 2020, ref
    #139)

-   Repeated sprints induce acute F-V profile shifts: decrease in F0
    (force) more than V0, causing profile to become more
    velocity-oriented with fatigue (Jimenez-Reyes et al. 2019, ref #128)

-   Tapering effects: de Lacey et al. (2014, ref #52) showed improved
    F-V profile balance after taper in rugby league players

**2. SPRINT BIOMECHANICS**

**2.1 Mechanical Determinants of Sprint Performance**

World-class sprinters are characterized by their mechanical
effectiveness, not just total force production. Key papers:

Rabita et al. 2015 (ref #59) - Sprint mechanics in world-class athletes:
Studied sprinters during 100m. Found that elite sprinters produce higher
horizontal GRF and maintain mechanical effectiveness (RF) across higher
speeds. Peak horizontal force in world-class = \~4.7 N/kg.

Morin et al. 2015 (ref #57) - \'Push more, brake less\': Elite sprinters
differentiate themselves by both pushing more horizontally and braking
less (lower horizontal braking impulse) compared to recreational
sprinters.

Morin et al. 2012 (ref #38) - 100m mechanical determinants: Body
mass-normalized measures of horizontal force application and mechanical
effectiveness are the primary mechanical determinants of 100m time, more
so than absolute force values.

**2.2 Phases of Sprint Acceleration**

**Initial Acceleration (0-10m / Steps 1-6)**

-   Highest horizontal force production occurs in first 1-3 steps

-   Body lean is greatest (50-60 degrees from horizontal)

-   Gluteal muscles play major role in horizontal force production
    (Morin et al. 2015, ref #64; Edouard et al. 2018, ref #120)

-   Hamstrings provide critical propulsive contribution; high horizontal
    hamstring force

-   RFmax achieved in steps 1-2; begins declining from step 3 onward

**Transition Phase (10-30m)**

-   Body gradually erects; RF declines with increasing velocity

-   Contribution of gluteals decreases relative to quads and hamstrings

-   Rate of force development becomes more important than peak force

-   von Lieres Und Wilkau et al. 2020 (ref #142): Both duration and
    magnitude of force application are critical; not just peak force

**Maximal Velocity Phase (\>30-40m)**

-   RF at maximal velocity = 25-35% (much less horizontal efficiency)

-   Vertical force dominates; must overcome gravity while maintaining
    speed

-   Swing leg mechanics become critical (Nagahara et al. 2019, ref
    #131): Hip flexor power important indicator

-   Hamstrings at high risk during terminal swing phase (peak
    lengthening velocity)

-   Mendiguchia et al. 2021 (ref #156): Running posture at maximal speed
    can be modified; has implications for both performance and injury

**2.3 Sprint Biomechanics and Hamstring Injury**

This is a major intersection of Morin\'s sprint biomechanics work and
injury prevention. Key mechanistic findings:

Morin et al. 2015 (ref #64) - Hamstrings\' major role in horizontal
force production: EMG and force analysis showed hamstrings are the
primary muscle group responsible for the horizontal push during early
sprint acceleration. This creates a dual role: propulsive during
acceleration AND at injury risk during high-speed running.

Edouard et al. 2021 (ref #161): Low horizontal force production capacity
is a RISK FACTOR for hamstring injury in football. Athletes with low F0
(horizontal) relative to body mass had higher injury incidence. This is
a prospective risk factor finding.

Bramah et al. 2024 (ref #181) - Opinion on sprint biomechanics and HSI:
Reviews the current evidence linking sprint mechanics to hamstring
strain injury. Key points: peak hamstring loading occurs at high-speed
running during terminal swing; anterior pelvic tilt increases hamstring
load; maintaining horizontal force capacity may protect against injury.

Romero et al. 2022 (ref #165): Fatigue-induced changes in sprint
biomechanics show high inter-individual variability. Group-level data
masks individual responses. Some athletes increase anterior pelvic tilt
under fatigue (increasing hamstring injury risk); others do not.

Prince et al. 2021 (ref #150): Nordic curls and other isolated hamstring
exercises are NOT specific to the sprint task. Force orientation during
nordic is predominantly vertical/distal, whereas sprinting requires
horizontal/proximal hamstring force. Specificity argument for why
isolated exercises may not transfer.

**2.4 Resisted Sprint Training**

Resisted sprint training (sled, elastic bands, uphill) is a major
applied area built directly on F-V profiling theory.

**Optimal Sled Load**

Cross et al. (2017, ref #99): Optimal load for maximizing power during
sled-resisted sprinting is approximately 75-80% of body mass on standard
athletic surfaces (grass, turf). This load maximizes the power output
integral across the sprint.

Cross et al. (2018, ref #104): Friction is the critical variable in sled
sprinting, not mass. Surface type dramatically alters the effective
loading. Grass has higher friction than track. Always account for
surface friction when prescribing sled loads.

Morin et al. 2017 (ref #95) - Very heavy sled training: Even loads \>75%
BM (up to 120% BM) improved horizontal force production in soccer
players after 4-week block. Heavy sleds shift training stimulus to the
force end of the F-V spectrum.

**Load Selection Based on F-V Profile**

-   Force-deficit athlete: Use heavier loads (60-100% BM), fewer reps,
    full recovery

-   Velocity-deficit athlete: Use lighter loads (10-20% BM) or assisted
    sprints, higher velocity execution

-   Balanced profile: Alternate heavy/light sled across training blocks

Cross et al. (2018, ref #112): Single-sprint vs. multi-trial methods for
computing F-V profile from resisted sprints. Multi-trial (different
loads across sessions) is more reliable but single maximal sprint method
is practical.

Petrakos et al. 2016 (ref #68): Systematic review of resisted sled
sprint training. Consistent improvements in acceleration performance
(5-10m times) with loads between 20-80% BM. Less effect on top speed.
Duration of training: minimum 4 weeks needed to see significant changes.

Le Scouarnec et al. 2022 (ref #162): Elastic resistance during repeated
sprint training. Progressive elastic resistance preserved sprint
mechanics better than fixed resistance and improved F-V profile balance
in young soccer players.

Delaney et al. (2021, ref #158): Uphill sprint profiling as alternative
to sled. Incline changes effective loading; steeper grades (5-10%)
target force end of spectrum. Practical tool for teams without sled
equipment.

**Cross et al. (2019, ref #126): Assessing Horizontal Force in Resisted
Sprinting**

Practical formula: Net horizontal force = (Sprint force from velocity
data) - (Sled friction force). Friction = sled mass x coefficient of
friction (surface-specific). Must calibrate for each surface type.

**2.5 In-Situ Acceleration-Speed Profiling (ASP)**

Morin et al. 2021 (ref #154): Proof of concept for computing individual
acceleration-speed profiles from GPS data collected during actual
training/match play. Extends the lab-based F-V profiling concept to
continuous field monitoring.

Method: Uses maximal acceleration values at each running speed from GPS
data collected over many sessions. Fits a linear relationship to
construct the acceleration-speed profile (equivalent to F-V profile in
meaningful units).

Clavel et al. 2023 (ref #168): Reliability of in-situ ASP in elite youth
soccer. Reliable after \~15 training sessions. Key finding: requires
sufficient maximal effort data points across the speed spectrum. Players
need to regularly produce maximal accelerations at high speeds.

Clavel et al. 2022 (ref #163): GPS devices provide concurrent valid F-V
profiles when players reach near-maximal efforts. 10 Hz GPS is the
practical minimum; higher sampling rates improve accuracy.

Vantieghem-Nicolas et al. 2023 (ref #174): K-AI wearable provides valid
and reliable sprint F-V profiles. Alternative to GPS for teams without
tracking systems.

**2.6 Sprint Asymmetry**

Lateral mechanical asymmetry during sprinting is a meaningful
performance and injury-risk variable.

-   Brown et al. (2017, ref #107): Kinetic sprint asymmetries on
    non-motorised treadmill in rugby union. Significant asymmetries
    exist even in elite athletes; not always pathological.

-   Girard et al. (2020, ref #140): Asymmetries during repeated
    treadmill sprints in elite female rugby sevens. Asymmetries increase
    with fatigue; may be injury risk marker.

-   Girard et al. (2017, ref #90): Lower limb mechanical asymmetry
    during repeated treadmill sprints. Defined thresholds for clinically
    meaningful asymmetry (\>10-15% interlimb difference).

-   Brown et al. (2017, ref #105): Targeted strength training can
    decrease asymmetry and increase sprint performance. Proof of concept
    study.

-   Brughelli et al. 2015 (ref #55): Asymmetry after hamstring injury in
    EPL players may persist beyond return to play. Sprint profiling can
    detect residual asymmetries.

**2.7 Fatigue Effects on Sprint Mechanics**

Repeated sprint fatigue produces consistent and predictable mechanical
changes:

-   Decrease in F0 (force capacity) more than V0 with repeated sprints

-   Decrease in RF (mechanical effectiveness) - sprinters become less
    horizontally efficient

-   Increase in contact time; decrease in stride frequency

-   Anterior pelvic tilt may increase under fatigue (injury risk)

Morin et al. 2011 (ref #26): During repeated sprints, force production
decreases but force application technique (RF) also deteriorates. Both
contribute to performance decline. Technical factor can explain 30-40%
of performance loss independent of metabolic fatigue.

Romero et al. 2022 (ref #165): High inter-individual variability in
fatigue responses. Some athletes show no change in pelvic tilt; others
show marked increases. Individualized fatigue monitoring needed.

Edouard et al. 2018 (ref #120): Under fatigue, gluteal muscle
compensation for hamstrings. Gluteals increase relative contribution to
horizontal force production when hamstrings are fatigued, potentially a
protective mechanism.

Girard et al. 2020 (ref #145): Repeated treadmill sprints - both early
and late acceleration phases affected by fatigue, but differently. Early
acceleration phase mechanics deteriorate faster (force production);
later phase (near Vmax) shows greater frequency decrements.

**3. HAMSTRING INJURY: PREVENTION, MONITORING, AND REHABILITATION**

**3.1 Multifactorial Risk Framework**

Morin\'s group operationalized a multifactorial approach where sprint
mechanical profiling is one key element alongside anatomy, strength, and
training load.

Edouard et al. 2024 (ref #180): Prospective cohort in professional
football using individualized multifactorial program. Components: sprint
F-V profiling, hamstring strength assessment, running posture analysis,
training load management, targeted exercise prescription. Result: lower
injury incidence vs historical controls.

**Risk Factor Categories**

-   Mechanical: Low horizontal force capacity (F0), high DRF (rapid
    decline in effectiveness), excessive anterior pelvic tilt at high
    speed

-   Structural: Low hamstring volume/cross-sectional area, low pennation
    angle, asymmetrical anatomy

-   Neuromuscular strength: Low eccentric hamstring strength (Nordic
    curl torque), imbalanced H:Q ratio

-   Training load: Sudden increases in sprint volume, insufficient
    recovery between sprint sessions

-   History: Previous hamstring strain is strongest single predictor

-   Posture: Anterior pelvic tilt increases hamstring length and loading
    during terminal swing

**3.2 Sprint F-V Profiling as Injury Risk Screening**

Edouard et al. 2021 (ref #161): Low horizontal force production during
sprinting is an independent risk factor for hamstring injury in
football. Athletes in the lowest tertile of F0 had significantly higher
injury rates. Prospective study design (n = professional football
players over one season).

Edouard et al. 2022 (ref #164): Comprehensive review confirming
sprinting as a key piece of hamstring injury risk management. Sprint
profiling should be integrated into routine assessment. Key metric: F0
(horizontal) and DRF.

Mendiguchia et al. (2016, ref #67): Case reports showing F-V profile
monitoring before, during, and after hamstring injury. Profile showed
significant decrease in F0 pre-injury (possible early warning signal).
Gradual restoration of F-V profile tracks rehabilitation progress.

**3.3 Return to Sprint After Hamstring Injury**

Mendiguchia et al. (2014, ref #47): Sprint F-V profile mechanical
properties during return to sport after hamstring injury. Complete
restoration of F0 and RF takes longer than resolution of symptoms.
Mechanical criteria should supplement clinical criteria for RTS.

Mendiguchia et al. (2017, ref #93): Multifactorial, criteria-based
progressive algorithm for hamstring treatment. Includes sprint profiling
milestones at each stage. Athletes progress through sprint speed targets
while monitoring mechanics.

Brughelli et al. (2015, ref #55): Asymmetry after hamstring injury may
persist at EPL return-to-play. Sprint profiling can detect residual
deficits missed by clinical tests.

**3.4 Hamstring-Specific Training**

Mendiguchia et al. (2020, ref #134): Sprint training vs. isolated
eccentric training for hamstring architecture and performance.
Sprint-specific training produced greater improvements in sprint
performance and maintained hamstring architecture better. Isolated
eccentric work (nordic) improved eccentric strength but had lower sprint
transfer.

Prince et al. (2021, ref #150): Force production specificity of isolated
hamstring exercises vs. sprinting. Nordicand similar exercises produce
predominantly vertical and distal hamstring force, whereas sprinting
requires horizontal and proximal force. Neither exercise alone fully
replicates sprint demands.

Hegyi et al. (2019, ref #124): Hip flexion angle affects nordic
hamstring torque and EMG. At 90 degrees hip flexion (upright position),
hamstring torque is higher than at 30 degrees. High-density EMG shows
biceps femoris long head is primary active muscle. Angle selection
matters for targeting specific portions of the hamstring group.

Mendiguchia et al. (2015, ref #62): Hamstring-emphasized neuromuscular
training improved strength AND sprint mechanics in football players.
Combined approach (eccentric strength + sprint-specific mechanics work)
superior to either alone.

Edouard et al. (2018, ref #120): Sprint acceleration mechanics under
fatigue: Gluteal muscles compensate for hamstrings during fatigued
sprinting. This compensatory mechanism may explain why gluteal strength
is also an injury risk factor.

**3.5 Pelvic Tilt and Running Posture**

Mendiguchia et al. (2020, ref #146): Training-induced changes in
anterior pelvic tilt and implications for hamstring injury. Athletes who
habitually train with excessive anterior pelvic tilt may have
chronically increased hamstring elongation and strain during high-speed
running.

Mendiguchia et al. (2021, ref #156): Maximal speed running posture CAN
be modified through targeted intervention. Running posture coaching and
specific exercises (hip flexor stretching, glute strengthening) can
reduce anterior pelvic tilt at high speed. Potential to reduce hamstring
injury risk and improve performance.

Edouard et al. (2021, ref #151): Lower limb injury location shifts from
posterior lower leg to hamstrings with increasing running velocity in
international athletics. Higher velocity demands = higher hamstring
injury proportion. Velocity-specific injury epidemiology.

**4. RUNNING MECHANICS: SPRING-MASS MODEL AND BIOMECHANICS**

**4.1 Spring-Mass Model Framework**

The spring-mass model represents the human body during running as a
point mass (body center of mass) supported by a massless, linear spring
(the leg). It is a useful simplification for analyzing running mechanics
at steady state.

Morin et al. (2005, ref #2): Original simple method for measuring leg
stiffness during running. Using only contact and flight time (measurable
with simple timing systems), leg stiffness can be calculated with
reasonable accuracy.

Morin et al. (2006, ref #4): Spring-mass characteristics during sprint
running correlate with performance. Lower DRF, higher kleg, and shorter
contact times associated with better sprint times. First demonstration
of spring-mass relevance in sprinting.

**4.2 Running Economy and Efficiency**

The spring-mass model connects to running economy: optimal leg stiffness
minimizes metabolic cost. Too low = excessive energy loss; too high =
less capacity to absorb and return energy.

-   Morin et al. 2011 (ref #22): Over 24-hour run, spring-mass behavior
    changes progressively. kleg decreases with fatigue; contact time
    increases; stride frequency decreases. Mechanical changes precede
    subjective exhaustion.

-   Pantoja et al. 2016 (ref #77): Masters athletes show similar running
    economy to young athletes despite age-related neuromuscular changes.
    Maintained through adaptations in spring-mass behavior.

-   Vernillo et al. 2017 (ref #89): Review of biomechanics of uphill and
    downhill running. kleg is lower downhill (more compliant) and higher
    uphill. Economy differs significantly across grades.

-   Millet et al. (2012, refs #35-36): During ultramarathon, economy may
    be \'sacrificed\' to maintain completion capacity. Spring-mass
    parameters degrade progressively; not a disadvantageous adaptation
    but a survival strategy.

**4.3 Fatigue and Running Mechanics**

Comprehensive body of work examining how prolonged and intense exercise
alters running mechanics:

-   Degache et al. (2016, ref #75): During world\'s most challenging
    mountain ultramarathon (UTMB), running mechanics show characteristic
    fatigue pattern: contact time increases, stride frequency decreases,
    kleg decreases. Changes are progressive and correlate with
    performance time.

-   Morin et al. (2011, ref #21): Mountain ultra-marathon induces
    specific spring-mass changes. Greater changes in kleg than in gross
    mechanics; reflects neuromuscular fatigue accumulation.

-   Morin et al. (2011, ref #33): High-intensity sprint fatigue does NOT
    alter subsequent submaximal running mechanics. Sprint fatigue is
    neuromuscular/metabolic but doesn\'t carry over to submaximal
    spring-mass behavior within minutes.

-   Giandolini et al. (2016, refs #79-80): Fatigue from prolonged graded
    running affects both central and peripheral neuromuscular function.
    Downhill running produces more peripheral fatigue (muscle damage);
    uphill more central (neural).

-   Delhaye et al. (2024, ref #187): First field data on submaximal F-V
    relationships during mountain ultramarathon. F-V profile measurable
    in the field during ultra-endurance events; shows progressive
    decline in force capacity.

**4.4 Foot Strike Pattern**

Significant research contribution on foot strike pattern effects on
running mechanics and injury risk:

Giandolini et al. (2013-2016, refs #41, #42, #44, #74): Series of papers
establishing that forefoot/midfoot striking reduces impact accelerations
compared to rearfoot striking. The reduction in peak tibial acceleration
is mechanically meaningful for injury risk reduction.

Van den Berghe et al. (2024, ref #186): Multi-cohort investigation of
peak tibial accelerations across foot strike patterns. Confirms rearfoot
striking produces higher tibial accelerations across different cohorts
and conditions. Effect is consistent and independent of other
biomechanical variables.

Giandolini et al. (2014, ref #44): Simple field method to identify foot
strike pattern using insole pressure sensors or accelerometers.
Practical screening tool for clinical and coaching contexts.

Giandolini et al. (2016, ref #74): In downhill trail running, foot
strike pattern affects axial AND transverse shock components
differently. Midfoot striking reduces axial shock but may not reduce all
transverse tibial loading.

Giandolini et al. (2017, ref #96): Foot strike pattern in downhill trail
running affects muscle activity and neuromuscular fatigue. Forefoot
striking increases calf muscle demand; rearfoot striking increases
quadriceps demand in downhill conditions.

**4.5 Trail Running and Ultramarathon**

-   Ehrström et al. (2018, ref #111): Short trail race performance:
    Technical terrain proficiency adds to classic endurance model.
    Running economy on flat does not fully predict trail race
    performance.

-   Degache et al. (2016, ref #75): Mechanical adaptations during UTMB
    (170km). Contact time +15-20% by end; kleg -20-30%. Functional but
    inefficient gait strategy.

-   Giandolini et al. (2016, ref #70): 110km ultramarathon induces
    significant increases in tibial impact acceleration. Neuromuscular
    fatigue progressively reduces shock attenuation.

-   Millet et al. (ref #20, 2011): 24h treadmill ultramarathon -
    physiological and mechanical correlates of performance. Key
    metabolic and mechanical predictors identified.

-   Vernillo et al. (2017, ref #89): Comprehensive review of
    uphill/downhill running biomechanics and physiology. Essential
    reference for trail running coaches.

**4.6 Foot and Ankle Function**

Tourillon et al. (2024, ref #185): Foot-ankle rebound jump test for
measuring reactive strength. Reliable test-retest; captures ankle
stiffness and reactive strength specific to the foot-ankle complex.

Roma et al. (2024, ref #184): Reliability of maximal voluntary toe
plantarflexion measurement. Standardized protocol provides reliable
baseline for foot rehabilitation and performance assessment.

Tourillon et al. (2024, ref #182): Foot muscle strength associated with
sprint acceleration, cutting, and jumping performance in high-level
athletes. Intrinsic foot muscle strength is a meaningful contributor to
athletic performance, not just an injury prevention variable.

Griffin et al. (2021, ref #160): Criteria-based rehabilitation protocol
for chronic mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy. Sprint F-V profiling
integrated as outcome measure in RCT protocol.

**5. TEAM SPORTS: PERFORMANCE MONITORING AND APPLICATION**

**5.1 Soccer/Football**

Extensive applied work in football/soccer contexts:

-   Nagahara et al. (2016, ref #78): Sprint mechanical properties
    impaired during actual soccer match. First proof-of-concept for
    real-game monitoring of sprint mechanics. Mechanical effectiveness
    (RF) decreases across match duration.

-   Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2019, ref #125): Sprint F-V profile
    differences between soccer and futsal players. Futsal players more
    force-oriented; soccer players more velocity-oriented.
    Sport-specific profile shapes emerge from training demands.

-   Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2020, ref #137): Seasonal changes in sprint
    F-V profile of elite soccer players. Pre-season = more force
    capacity; mid-season = shift toward velocity. Informs periodization
    of physical work.

-   Buchheit et al. (2014, ref #53): Mechanical determinants of
    acceleration and maximal sprint speed in young soccer players. Both
    F0 and V0 are important; V0 more predictive of Vmax, F0 more
    predictive of 10m time.

-   Edouard et al. (2024, ref #180): Individualized multifactorial
    hamstring injury prevention in professional football. Sprint F-V
    profiling central to screening and monitoring.

-   Romero et al. (2022, ref #165): Individual variability in
    fatigue-induced sprint mechanics changes in football. Group data
    misleading; individual monitoring essential.

-   Lahti et al. (2020, ref #148): Heavy resisted sprint training in
    professional soccer. Changes in sprint performance and sagittal
    plane kinematics. Resisted training shifts posture toward more
    forward lean; may improve F-V profile.

**5.2 Rugby**

Significant body of work in rugby union and sevens contexts:

-   Cross et al. (2015, ref #56): Sprint mechanical properties in elite
    rugby union vs. rugby league. Position-specific differences;
    forwards more force-oriented, backs more velocity-oriented.

-   Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2019, ref #128): Changes in sprint mechanics
    during repeated sprints in elite rugby sevens. Progressive decline
    in F0 and RF; V0 more maintained. Fatigue-related mechanical changes
    are sprint-specific.

-   Lahti et al. (2020, ref #139): In-season assisted and resisted
    sprint training in professional rugby. Individualized velocity-based
    approach; monitored with F-V profiling. Individual responders
    identified.

-   Marrier et al. (2017-2018, refs #100, #114, #122, #123): Series on
    rugby sevens periodization, tapering, supercompensation. Sprint F-V
    profile used as physical performance indicator across season.

-   Brown et al. (2017, ref #107): Sprint asymmetries in rugby union on
    non-motorised treadmill. Position-specific asymmetry patterns; backs
    show more symmetrical sprint mechanics than forwards.

-   Glaise et al. (2023, ref #172): Opposition skill efficiency in
    professional rugby union related to horizontal force production
    capacity. Higher F0 associated with better ability to beat
    defenders. Practical team sport performance link.

**5.3 Metabolic Power and Sprint Demands in Team Sports**

A distinct line of work on metabolic power during team sport activity:

Osgnach et al. (2023-2024, refs #171, #178): Mechanical and metabolic
power in accelerated running for team sports. Extends the sprint
energetics model to the variable-acceleration context of team sports.
Key framework for quantifying true physiological demands.

di Prampero et al. (2023, ref #175): Mechanical and metabolic power
during 100m dash. Theoretical framework linking mechanical efficiency to
energy expenditure during maximal sprinting.

Buchheit et al. (2015, ref #63): Metabolic power vs. GPS distance - is
metabolic power monitoring worth it for soccer? Findings: metabolic
power provides additional information beyond distance/speed,
particularly for high-acceleration efforts. But GPS accuracy limits
precision.

**5.4 Acceleration-Speed Profile In-Situ Applications**

The in-situ ASP from GPS tracking represents the practical extension of
lab-based F-V profiling to team sport contexts:

The core method (Morin et al. 2021, ref #154) requires:

-   GPS data from many training/match sessions (10-20 sessions minimum
    for reliability)

-   Players must regularly reach near-maximal efforts at multiple speeds

-   Maximum acceleration at each speed band is extracted

-   Linear regression fitted to acceleration-speed data gives
    F0-equivalent and V0-equivalent

Clavel et al. (2022-2023, refs #163, #168): GPS-derived ASP is
concurrently valid and reliable in elite youth soccer. At least 15
sessions of data needed for stable estimates. 10 Hz GPS minimum; higher
rates preferred.

Practical applications: Track seasonal changes in mechanical profile
without interrupting training; monitor return from injury; identify
players with force vs. velocity deficits for targeted training.

**6.2 Force-Velocity Profile Reliability Data**

Key reliability findings across measurement methods:

-   Samozino et al. (2016, ref #72): Simple sprint F-V method has CV \<
    5% for F0, V0, Pmax across test-retest sessions when performed on
    same surface and conditions.

-   Cross et al. (2018, ref #112): Single-sprint F-V method: ICC \> 0.85
    for all main variables. Comparable to multi-trial methods in
    reliability.

-   Clavel et al. (2023, ref #168): In-situ ASP reliability: CV 5-12%
    depending on number of sessions included. Minimum 15 sessions
    recommended.

-   Vantieghem-Nicolas et al. (2023, ref #174): K-AI wearable: ICC \>
    0.90 for F0, V0; moderate-high concurrent validity vs radar.

-   Fornasier-Santos et al. (2022, ref #166): Multi-system comparison
    shows position-time and velocity-time methods give similar F-V
    outputs; velocity-time generally slightly more reliable.

-   Samozino et al. (2022, ref #167): Concept, method, and measurement
    errors in F-V-P profiling. The concept (theoretical model) is sound;
    measurement error comes primarily from data collection quality, not
    the computational method.

**6.3 Jump Performance Measurement**

-   Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2017, ref #84): Jump F-V method (loaded jumps)
    valid and reliable. ICC \> 0.92 for F0, V0, Pmax across 3 sessions.

-   Morin et al. (2019, ref #129): Jump height unreliable as power
    indicator. Must profile F-V relationship to understand neuromuscular
    capacities.

-   Cross et al. (2021, ref #152): Countermovement effect on force
    production depends on extension velocity. CMJ vs. SJ produce
    different F-V profiles; not interchangeable for profiling.

**6.4 Running Mechanics Measurement**

-   Gindre et al. (2016, ref #65): Myotest IMU for stride kinematics:
    Reliable for stride length, frequency, contact time. Smaller
    validity for vertical oscillation.

-   Balsalobre et al. (2017, ref #94): iPhone app for running mechanics:
    Valid for contact time and stride frequency; smaller validity for
    vertical stiffness.

-   Buchheit et al. (2015, ref #61): GPS-embedded accelerometers for
    stride variables and vertical stiffness: Preliminary insights;
    requires field validation. Practical but less accurate than
    lab-grade systems.

-   Girard et al. (2016, ref #71): Treadmill sprint running mechanics:
    intrasession ICC \> 0.85 for all major variables; intersession ICC
    0.70-0.92. Treadmill data is reliable for tracking changes within
    and between sessions.

-   Harper et al. (2020, ref #144): Radar technology for maximal
    horizontal deceleration measurement. Reliable and sensitive to
    training-induced changes. Novel application of radar to deceleration
    assessment.

**6.5 Nordic Hamstring and Strength Testing**

-   Hegyi et al. (2019, ref #124): Hip flexion angle is critical
    variable in Nordic hamstring exercise. Must standardize angle for
    reliable repeated measures.

-   Tourillon et al. (2024, ref #185): Foot-ankle rebound jump: CV \< 5%
    test-retest; ICC \> 0.90. Reliable measure of reactive strength in
    the foot-ankle complex.

-   Roma et al. (2024, ref #184): Toe plantarflexion measurement:
    reliable with standardized protocol. Mean differences \< 5% across
    test-retest.

-   Junge et al. (2020, ref #143): F-V imbalance in leg extension
    negatively impacts sprint performance in ball-game players. Sprint
    and jump F-V profiling should complement each other in comprehensive
    assessment.

**7.2 Improving Horizontal Force Application**

Hicks et al. (Strength Cond J, ref #136): Practical recommendations for
improving mechanical effectiveness during sprint acceleration:

-   Start position: forward lean, foot placement behind center of mass

-   First step: aggressive push-back and down; minimize upward
    displacement

-   Arm action: powerful rear-drive action to counter forward leg drive

-   Cues: \'push the ground back\', \'stay long\', \'drive the heel
    down\'

-   Feedback: Use video or force plate feedback on RF% to show athletes
    their effectiveness

Morin et al. 2015 (ref #57): Elite sprinters \'push more and brake
less\'. Coaching emphasis on horizontal propulsion (not just force) is
evidence-based.

**7.3 Sprint Training Programming**

Key programming principles from the literature:

-   Frequency: Minimum 2 maximal sprint sessions per week to maintain
    F-V profile; 3 sessions if actively trying to shift profile

-   Sprint dose: Morin et al. 2011 (ref #23): Optimal sprint dose for
    RSA assessment is 6-8 sprints of 30-40m. More sprints produce
    diminishing returns and greater fatigue.

-   Recovery: Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2020, ref #128): F-V profile
    substantially altered after 6+ maximal sprints. Allow minimum 48
    hours for mechanical profile recovery.

-   Load progression: Lloria-Varella et al. (2023, ref #169): 2 weeks of
    sprint interval training shifts F-V profile significantly. Short
    training blocks can produce measurable profile changes.

-   Resisted sprint periodization: Cross et al. (2018, ref #113):
    Training at maximal power in resisted sprints (optimal load \~75-80%
    BM) for 4-6 weeks effectively shifts force-velocity balance toward
    force end.

**7.4 Return to Sport Sprint Progression**

Derived from the hamstring injury monitoring work:

Mendiguchia et al. (2014, ref #47) progression framework:

-   Stage 1: Sub-maximal running (60-70% Vmax); monitor RF; target RF \>
    40%

-   Stage 2: High-speed running (80-85% Vmax); F-V profile should show
    recovery toward baseline

-   Stage 3: Near-maximal sprinting (90-95% Vmax); F-V profile within
    10% of pre-injury baseline

-   Stage 4: Full sprint efforts (100%); F-V profile fully restored;
    symmetry within 5-10%

Criteria: Return to sport when: (1) F0 within 10% of
contralateral/pre-injury; (2) Asymmetry \< 10%; (3) RF values within 10%
of pre-injury; (4) Full sprint velocity achieved without compensation.

**7.5 Periodization Insights from Rugby Sevens Work**

Marrier et al. series (refs #88, #100, #114, #122, #123) provides
detailed insight into how F-V profile changes across a rugby sevens
season:

-   Pre-season: F0 highest (heavy gym work); V0 lower; profile
    force-oriented

-   Mid-season: F0 maintained; V0 increases with sprint training;
    profile moves toward optimal balance

-   Taper: Both F0 and V0 may increase slightly; power output peaks

-   Post-competition: F0 can decline if sprint training volume drops

Key finding (Marrier et al. 2017, ref #100): Supercompensation of
physical qualities during taper occurs in 7-14 day window. Sprint F-V
profile improves most in first 7-10 days of taper. Avoid over-tapering
(\>14 days) which can reduce F0.

**8. ADVANCED TOPICS AND EMERGING AREAS**

**8.1 Bilateral Deficit in Force-Velocity Context**

Samozino et al. (2014, ref #43): F-V properties contribution to
bilateral deficit during ballistic push-off. Bilateral deficit is partly
explained by different F-V profiles in bilateral vs. unilateral
contexts, not just neural inhibition.

Cross et al. (2021, ref #152): Countermovement effect on force
production is velocity-dependent. CMJ produces higher force at lower
velocities than squat jump. When profiling, must standardize whether CMJ
or SJ is used.

Rivière et al. (2023, ref #170): Exploring the low force-high velocity
domain of the F-V relationship in acyclic lower-limb extensions.
Standard profiling undersamples the high velocity end; multiple jump
conditions including unloaded are needed to fully characterize V0.

**8.2 Advanced Footwear Technology**

Mason et al. (2024, ref #183): Sex-specific evolutions in elite running
performance and advanced footwear. Women\'s performances improved more
than men\'s since carbon-plated shoes introduction. Possible
differential biomechanical benefit by sex.

Mason et al. (2023, ref #176): Advanced footwear impact on elite sprint
performance. Carbon-plated sprint spikes may contribute to recent sprint
world record improvements. Analysis of performance trends before and
after technology introduction.

**8.3 Clinical Populations**

Morin\'s F-V framework extends to clinical populations:

-   Passos-Monteiro et al. (2019, ref #132): Sprint exercise for
    Parkinson\'s disease (mild-moderate). Feasible and produces
    measurable biomechanical outputs. Sprint F-V profiling possible in
    clinical populations.

-   Fanget et al. (2020, ref #133): Force production capacities in
    coronary artery disease patients vs healthy on cycle ergometer. F-V
    profiling applicable in cardiac rehabilitation context.

-   Rambaud et al. (2017, ref #91): Return to sport criteria after ACL
    reconstruction (CR\'TAL study). Sprint F-V profiling included as
    outcome measure in prospective observational study.

**8.4 Environmental Effects on Sprint Mechanics**

Series of studies by Girard, Brocherie, Morin, and Millet on
environmental sprint biomechanics:

-   Hypoxia: Brocherie et al. (2016, ref #76): Repeated treadmill
    sprints in normobaric hypoxia reduce mechanical outputs compared to
    normoxia. Contact time increases; flight time decreases. Sprint
    mechanics deteriorate faster under hypoxic conditions.

-   Heat: Girard et al. (2017, ref #87): Heat stress during repeated
    treadmill sprints. Higher ambient temperature accelerates mechanical
    deterioration. Contact time increases earlier; stride frequency
    declines faster.

-   Combined hypoxia + heat: Girard et al. (2016, ref #66): Mechanical
    alterations during repeated sprints in hot vs. hypoxic environments.
    Heat is more detrimental to mechanics than hypoxia; combined
    conditions worst.

-   Altitude: di Prampero et al. (2021, ref #155): Energetics of 100m
    dash at altitude. Reduced air resistance at altitude improves sprint
    performance; mechanical and metabolic model applied.

-   Live High Train Low: Girard et al. (2017, ref #98): LHTL hypoxic
    training does not alter running mechanics in elite team sport
    players. Hypoxic adaptation is metabolic, not mechanical.

**8.5 IMU-Based Sprint Assessment**

Nagahara et al. (2019, ref #131): IMU-based hip flexion test as sprint
performance indicator. Hip flexor power measured via IMU correlates with
10-40m split times. Field-practical alternative to force platform
assessment.

Growing area: Multiple labs validating IMU for sprint mechanical
profiling. Key challenge: accurate computation of velocity from
acceleration data (drift error). Position integration from IMU requires
careful signal processing.

**8.6 Energetics of Sprint Running**

di Prampero et al. series (refs #3, #155, #175, #178, #179):
Comprehensive energetic framework for sprint running extending from the
1960s-2024:

-   Original model: sprint running equivalent to uphill running; slope
    proportional to forward acceleration

-   Mechanical power = product of horizontal force and velocity of the
    center of mass

-   Metabolic power derivable from mechanical power via efficiency
    factor (eta \~0.25 for sprinting)

-   Applied to team sports: allows estimation of metabolic cost of every
    acceleration/deceleration event during match play

-   Controversy (ref #179): Ettema\'s critique about internal work
    overestimation addressed and rebutted

**9. KEY FORMULAS AND EQUATIONS**

**9.1 Sprint F-V Profile Computation**

From velocity-time data during a single maximal sprint acceleration:

Step 1: Net horizontal force at each instant

Fh(t) = m x a(t) + Faero(t)

where Faero = 0.5 x rho x Cd x Af x v(t)\^2

Step 2: Ratio of Forces at each instant

RF(t) = Fh(t) / Ftot(t)

Ftot = sqrt(Fh\^2 + Fv\^2); Fv estimated from spring-mass model

Step 3: Linear F-V relationship

F0 = y-intercept of linear regression of Fh vs velocity

V0 = x-intercept of the same regression

Pmax = F0 x V0 / 4

Step 4: Mechanical effectiveness

RFmax = RF at first step (lowest velocity)

DRF = slope of RF vs velocity (always negative)

**9.2 Optimal F-V Profile**

Samozino et al. (2021, ref #157):

Optimal slope (SFopt) depends on: body mass (m), height (h), F0, V0,
gravitational acceleration (g)

FV-Imbalance (%) = \[(SFactual - SFopt) / SFopt\] x 100

Positive = force deficit; Negative = velocity deficit

**9.3 Spring-Mass Model**

Morin et al. (2005, ref #2):

kleg = Fmax / delta_L

kvert = Fmax / delta_z

Fmax = (pi/2) x m x g x (1 + (pi x tf) / (2 x tc))

delta_z = g x tc\^2 / 8 + g x tf\^2 / (2 x pi\^2) x \... \[see original
paper\]

**9.4 Jump F-V Profile (Simple Method)**

From Samozino et al. (2008, ref #8) and Jimenez-Reyes et al. (2017, ref
#84):

For each jump load: F = (m x g x h) + (0.5 x m x v0\^2) / h

where h = jump height, v0 = mean push-off velocity

Plot F vs v0 across multiple loads; linear regression gives F0 and V0

**9.5 Sled Load and Friction**

Cross et al. (2017, ref #104):

Effective horizontal load from sled = sled mass x g x mu (coefficient of
friction)

Net horizontal sprint force = unresisted sprint force - sled friction
force

Optimal power load = approximately 78% of BM on grass; less on track

Surface-specific friction coefficients (approximate):

-   Grass: mu \~ 0.45-0.55

-   Artificial turf: mu \~ 0.35-0.45

-   Track (rubber): mu \~ 0.25-0.35

-   Tarmac/concrete: mu \~ 0.30-0.40

**10.3 Repeated Sprint Assessment**

Morin et al. (2011, ref #23): Recommended protocol for repeated sprint
ability with mechanical monitoring:

-   6-10 sprints of 30-40m with 25-30 seconds passive recovery

-   Capture velocity-time trace for each sprint

-   Compute F-V profile for each sprint

-   Track decline in F0, RF, and sprint time across repetitions

-   Fatigue index = % decline from sprint 1 to last sprint

**10.4 Hamstring Risk Screening Protocol**

Derived from Edouard et al. (2021, ref #161) and Mendiguchia et al.
(2024, ref #180):

-   Sprint F-V profile: measure F0 (horizontal); flag if \< 6.5 N/kg
    (approximately)

-   Nordic hamstring: measure eccentric torque at 90 degrees hip, 30
    degrees knee flexion; flag if \< 250 Nm

-   Running posture: video analysis at 85-90% Vmax; measure anterior
    pelvic tilt; flag if \> 10-15 degrees

-   Asymmetry: measure interlimb difference in sprint mechanics; flag if
    \> 10-15%

-   Training load: monitor weekly sprint volume and acute:chronic ratio;
    flag if A:C \> 1.5
